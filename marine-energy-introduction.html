Marine Energy

The ocean offers several ways to do useful work. Let's look at where that energy comes from, how we can capture it, and what makes a device practical.

Start with the resource

Here, marine energy means energy from waves, tides, river and ocean currents, and differences in water temperature or salinity. Offshore wind belongs to the wider offshore-renewables picture, but it takes its energy from air, so we keep it separate in these lessons. [DOE][scope]

Waves

Winds transfer energy to surface waves. Exposed coasts can offer a strong resource, but the waves also change with the weather and can impose severe storm loads.

Tidal streams

Tides drive currents through channels and straits. Their timing is predictable, although the flow varies through each tidal cycle and between spring and neap tides.

Tidal range

A barrage or lagoon can use a water-level difference to drive turbines. This is different from putting a free-stream turbine in a tidal current.

Ocean and river currents

A turbine can also use a flowing river or an ocean current. A useful site needs the right speed, depth, access, and environmental conditions; steady power is never automatic.

Ocean temperature

OTEC uses warm surface water and colder deep water as the heat source and sink for a heat engine. Tropical sites with deep-water access are the usual candidates.

Salinity gradients

Mixing fresh water and salt water releases free energy. Membrane systems can recover part of it, but pretreatment, fouling, and pumping matter.

Follow the resource and mechanism descriptions in [DOE resource assessment][EIA tidal power][EIA OTEC][Yip & Elimelech].

A resource estimate is not an electricity forecast. Theoretical potential describes the resource. Technical potential adds a technology's limits. Practical potential also accounts for economic, environmental, and regulatory constraints. Delivered power still depends on the actual system and its losses. [definitions]

Why do regular waves and irregular seas use different height measures?

A regular wave has one height H and period T. An irregular sea contains many components; its spectral significant height is Hm₀ = 4√m₀. Its mean energy is ρgHm₀²/16, while a regular wave uses ρgH²/8. Don't put Hm₀ into the regular-wave formula and expect the same answer. Try the spectral wave lab to see the distinction.

Why does doubling current speed make such a difference?

At fixed rotor area and power coefficient, the model scales with speed cubed: twice the speed gives eight times the rotor power. A real turbine may change its control settings or reach its rated output before that happens.

Does a 20°C temperature difference mean 20% efficiency?

No. The Carnot limit uses absolute temperatures in kelvin. It is an ideal upper bound; real OTEC output is lower after heat-transfer limitations, pumping, and other losses.

From a resource to useful power

Choose the mechanism

A buoy, an air chamber, a flap, and a current turbine interact with water differently. The Device Atlas shows the main tradeoffs.

Follow the energy

Track incident resource power, absorbed power, mechanical or pneumatic power, gross electricity, and net delivered output separately. A power take-off, or PTO, transfers the device's captured energy into a usable form.

Plan for the whole life

Moorings, foundations, cables, corrosion, fatigue, biofouling, and access for repairs all affect whether a promising device becomes a useful system.

Control can improve energy capture, but it must respect loads and operating limits. Reliability, installation, and maintenance belong in the design from the start. [PNNL engineering]

What could the power be used for?

Applications include grid supply, remote-community microgrids, desalination, and ocean observing. The right choice depends on the load as well as the resource. A small sensor and an island grid need very different systems. [DOE applications]

People and the environment are part of the design

Ask what a particular device and site could change: underwater sound, animal encounters, habitats, electromagnetic fields around cables, and local flow or sediment patterns. Effects depend on the design, location, and scale of deployment. Evidence from small deployments cannot settle every question about large arrays. [OES/PNNL synthesis]

It also helps to involve communities, fishers, navigation interests, and other ocean users early. Those conversations can shape siting, access, monitoring, and the way a project is operated. [engineering context]

How close is a technology to everyday use?

A technology readiness level, or TRL, describes how far a particular system has been demonstrated. It doesn't, by itself, tell you whether that system is affordable or ready for years of reliable operation. Ask which design was tested, at what scale, for how long, and in what conditions. [TRL definitions]

The Device Atlas readiness section puts published sector ranges in context. For named projects, use the IEA-OES annual report and check the date of the evidence.
