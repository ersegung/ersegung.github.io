Ocean Waves Lab

Change the wave height, period, and depth. Then connect the surface motion to particle velocities, pressure, and the energy carried by the waves.

Start with one regular wave

The drawing stretches the depth scale and magnifies displacement to make the motion visible. Surface and particle displacement use the same magnification; the numerical values below stay in SI units. Particle paths are first-order oscillations; this model has no Stokes drift.

Look below the surface

The probe evaluates the same wave at x = 0. Dynamic pressure means the wave-induced change from hydrostatic pressure. At z = 0, it is a linearized mean-surface value; a real fixed sensor there would not stay underwater throughout the wave.

Follow the energy

Hold period and depth fixed and wave power grows with height squared. Change the depth and the group velocity changes too. The power is incident flux per metre of wave crest, before a device captures any of it.

Now try an irregular sea

This is a separate example. Here the height slider means spectral significant height Hm₀, and the period slider means peak period Tp. Those are not the height and period of the regular wave above.

m₀ = ∫ S(f) df   ·   Hm₀ = 4√m₀   ·   P = ρg ∫ S(f) Cg(f,h) df

We normalize a finite band of a Pierson–Moskowitz-shaped spectrum to your chosen Hm₀. Each component uses the finite-depth group velocity. The 120-second record is one synthetic realization; its measured wave heights can differ from long-record statistics.

Keep the assumptions in view

These are linear gravity-wave models in constant depth, with a flat impermeable seabed, no current, and inviscid, incompressible, irrotational flow. Real sites can need nonlinear waves, breaking, directional spreading, currents, reflection, and changing bathymetry.

The regular-wave warning uses H/L = 0.142 tanh(kh) as the Miche screening limit. The additional ka and a/h checks at 0.1 are conservative teaching choices. None is an exact prediction of real-world breaking. In an irregular sea, individual waves can exceed Hm₀, so a regular-wave limit must not be treated as an exact limit on significant height.
